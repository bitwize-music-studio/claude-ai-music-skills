# Loretta Lynn — Deep Dive

## Overview

Loretta Lynn (1932-2022) was a Kentucky-born country singer and songwriter whose plain-spoken songs about marriage, cheating and drinking husbands, motherhood, birth control, divorce and war spoke for working-class women in a way country radio had not heard. A coal miner's daughter from eastern Kentucky, married at 15, she taught herself guitar and wrote her own material, starting with "I'm a Honky Tonk Girl" (1960). Produced by Owen Bradley at Decca, she had sixteen No. 1 country hits between 1966 and 1978, including duets with Conway Twitty, and in 1972 became the first woman named CMA Entertainer of the Year. Her autobiography became the 1980 film *Coal Miner's Daughter*. Late in life she made *Van Lear Rose* (2004) with Jack White, which won two Grammys, and a run of traditional albums produced at the Cash Cabin.

**Genre positioning:** Traditional country, honky-tonk, 1960s-70s Nashville; Appalachian folk and gospel underneath. AP: songs about sex, cheating husbands, divorce and birth control with "a defiant, tough female persona". The New York Times called her songs "three-minute morality plays".

**Commercial scale:** Sixteen No. 1 country singles (eleven solo, five with Conway Twitty); *Don't Come Home A-Drinkin'* (1967) was the first album by a female country singer to be certified Gold. Three Grammys from eighteen nominations, plus a Lifetime Achievement Award (2010). Country Music Hall of Fame (1988), Kennedy Center Honors (2003), Presidential Medal of Freedom (2013). *Full Circle* (2016) reached No. 19 on the Billboard 200, her highest.

**Key quote:** "I didn't write for the men; I wrote for us women." — Loretta Lynn, 2016

## Background

- **Born**: Loretta Webb, 14 April 1932, Butcher Hollow (Butcher Holler), Kentucky, in a one-room log cabin; second of eight children. Her father worked the Van Lear coal mines
- **Marriage**: Married Oliver "Doolittle" (or "Mooney") Lynn in January 1948, at 15. They moved to Custer, Washington, where he bought her "a $17 Sears guitar". Many of her songs drew on the marriage: "Those songs was true to life. We fought hard, and we loved hard"
- **First record**: Signed to Zero Records of Vancouver in February 1960. "I'm a Honky Tonk Girl", recorded in Los Angeles with Speedy West on steel, reached No. 14; the Lynns mailed records to stations and drove to radio stations, sleeping in their car
- **Nashville**: The Wilburn Brothers put her in their road show and got her signed to Decca, where Owen Bradley produced her "fifty-plus hits" (Country Music Hall of Fame). Grand Ole Opry debut in 1960; member from September 1962. The Wilburns' company held her publishing for decades, until a lawsuit settled it
- **Duets**: Three albums with Ernest Tubb; from 1971 one of country's most successful duet teams with Conway Twitty (CMA Vocal Duo of the Year 1972-1975)
- **Later career**: *Van Lear Rose* with Jack White (2004); from 2009 recordings with John Carter Cash at the Cash Cabin, released by Legacy (2016-2021), co-produced with her daughter Patsy Lynn Russell
- **Died**: 4 October 2022, aged 90, at her ranch in Hurricane Mills, Tennessee

## Sonic Evolution Summary

| Era | Releases | Sound |
|-----|----------|-------|
| 1960 | "I'm a Honky Tonk Girl" | West Coast honky-tonk: steel guitar, shuffle, a young twangy voice |
| 1962-1978 | Decca/MCA singles and albums with Owen Bradley | Nashville studio country: steel guitar (Hal Rugg), lead guitar (Grady Martin), piano (Floyd Cramer, Pig Robbins), the Jordanaires on backing vocals; short, punchy two-and-a-half-minute songs; fiddle and banjo on "Coal Miner's Daughter" |
| 1971-1981 | Duets with Conway Twitty | Country duets: call-and-response and harmony |
| 2004 | *Van Lear Rose* | Raw, loud, live-sounding: a young rock band with pedal steel, fiddle and banjo, recorded on analog eight-track |
| 2016-2021 | *Full Circle*, *Wouldn't It Be Great*, *Still Woman Enough* | Acoustic string band: mandolin, banjo, fiddle, upright bass, autoharp, pedal steel; Appalachian ballads and re-recorded hits |

**Tempo range:** Spotify-derived readings show two feels: shuffles and two-steps read at about 75-79 BPM (or double), and brisk songs at about 134-147 BPM. Most of the classic singles run 2:10-3:00.

## Musical Analysis

### The Bradley Sound

Owen Bradley's Nashville sessions put the singer "out front" (Country Music Hall of Fame) over a crack studio band: Hal Rugg's steel guitar, Grady Martin's lead guitar, Ray Edenton and Pete Wade on rhythm guitar, Floyd Cramer or Hargus "Pig" Robbins on piano, Harold Bradley, Junior Huskey or Bob Moore on bass, Buddy Harman on drums, and the Jordanaires singing backing parts. Unlike Bradley's smoother Nashville Sound records for other singers, Lynn's kept the steel guitar throughout, and fiddle (Johnny Gimble) and banjo appear on key records. The songs are short, often under two and a half minutes, with a quick intro and no wasted bars.

### Her Voice

A "Kentucky drawl", a "tensely coiled vibrato" and "deep reserves of power" (The New York Times); Bradley likened her to "a female Hank Williams". The delivery is direct and conversational, sharp on the defiant songs and tender on the ballads.

### Songwriting and Themes

She wrote most of her own hits, which was unusual for a woman in 1960s Nashville. Her method: "When I write a song, the melody just comes in my mind to fit that song… I let the song come to me." The themes:
- **Marriage and cheating**: "Don't Come Home A-Drinkin'", "You Ain't Woman Enough", "Fist City", "Rated 'X'" (the stigma divorced women faced)
- **Motherhood and birth control**: "One's on the Way", "The Pill"
- **War**: "Dear Uncle Sam", one of the first country songs about the Vietnam War, sung by a wife whose husband is sent overseas
- **Home and class**: "Coal Miner's Daughter", "You're Lookin' at Country", "Van Lear Rose"

Several of her songs were banned by radio stations: "Rated 'X'", "Wings Upon Your Horns" and "The Pill"; she said eight in all. "I could see that other women was goin' through the same thing, 'cause I worked the clubs."

### The Feminist Question

She is widely called a feminist icon, but she rejected the label ("I'm not a big fan of Women's Liberation", 1976); historians describe her as a voice of working-class Southern women who tackled sexism on her own terms. Country Music Hall of Fame CEO Kyle Young called her "brash and brave".

### Genre Classification

- **Traditional country / honky-tonk**: The primary label
- **Nashville country, 1960s-70s**: The Bradley-produced hits
- **Country duets**: With Conway Twitty and Ernest Tubb
- **Appalachian folk and gospel**: Her roots, and the late Cash Cabin albums
- **Roots rock / alt-country**: *Van Lear Rose*

## Reception and Influence

- **Jack White**: The White Stripes dedicated *White Blood Cells* (2001) to her and recorded "Rated X". In 2022 he said: "I thought she was the greatest female singer-songwriter of the 20th century. I still believe that."
- **Kacey Musgraves**: "Two of my biggest influences would be Loretta Lynn and Dolly Parton"; she sang "Coal Miner's Daughter" in the 2023 Grammys In Memoriam
- **Miranda Lambert, Sheryl Crow**: Re-recorded "Coal Miner's Daughter" with her (2010)
- **Tanya Tucker, Margo Price, Reba McEntire, Carrie Underwood**: Duet partners on *Still Woman Enough*
- **Covers**: Elvis Costello ("Honky Tonk Girl"), Neko Case ("Rated X"), Paramore and Martina McBride ("You Ain't Woman Enough")
- **Honours for "Coal Miner's Daughter"**: Grammy Hall of Fame (1998), National Recording Registry (2009); No. 14 in Rolling Stone's 200 Greatest Country Songs

- **"I'm a Honky Tonk Girl"** (1960) — The first record: West Coast honky-tonk with steel guitar
- **"Don't Come Home A-Drinkin'"** (1966) — First No. 1; a wife's warning to a drinking husband
- **"You Ain't Woman Enough"** (1966) — Confident put-down of a rival
- **"Fist City"** (1968) — Tough, funny warning to another woman
- **"Coal Miner's Daughter"** (1970) — Autobiography with steel, fiddle and banjo
- **"After the Fire Is Gone"** (with Conway Twitty, 1971) — Grammy-winning cheating duet
- **"One's on the Way"** (1971) — Overwhelmed mother's comic lament
- **"Rated 'X'"** (1972) — The stigma of divorce; banned by some stations
- **"The Pill"** (1975) — Birth-control anthem; her most controversial record

- **"Van Lear Rose"** (2004) — Title song named for the Van Lear coal country of her childhood
