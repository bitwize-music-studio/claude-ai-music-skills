# Dan Fogelberg — Deep Dive

## Overview

Dan Fogelberg (1951-2007) was an American singer, songwriter and multi-instrumentalist from Peoria, Illinois, whose warm high tenor, stacked harmonies, acoustic guitar and orchestral arrangements defined a strand of 1970s-80s soft rock, folk rock and country rock. Between 1974 and 1984 he made a run of Platinum albums and soft-rock hits: "Part of the Plan", "Longer", "Same Old Lang Syne", "Hard to Say", "Run for the Roses" and "Leader of the Band", the tribute to his father, a high-school band director, that is often called the definitive father song. He played most of the instruments on many of his records, moved to the Colorado mountains, made a bluegrass album and an environmental concept album, and died of prostate cancer at 56.

**Genre positioning:** Singer-songwriter, soft rock, folk rock, country rock; bluegrass on *High Country Snows*. Bruce Eder: "a sublimely beautiful melding of country-rock with the personal level of a singer/songwriter". Critics were often harsh at the time (Dave Marsh: "smooth post-rock cocktail-lounge Muzak"); later writers have reappraised him, and Garth Brooks says he "changed my life".

**Commercial scale:** Seven albums reached the US Top 25, including *Phoenix* (No. 3), *The Innocent Age* (No. 6) and *Twin Sons of Different Mothers* (No. 8); several are certified Platinum or double Platinum. "Longer" reached No. 2 on the Hot 100 and No. 1 Adult Contemporary; he had four AC No. 1s between 1980 and 1984.

**Key quote:** "If I wrote only one song, it would be 'Leader of the Band.'" — Dan Fogelberg

## Background

- **Born**: Daniel Grayling Fogelberg, 13 August 1951, Peoria, Illinois; the youngest of three sons. His mother, Margaret, was a Scottish-born, classically trained musician
- **His father**: Lawrence Fogelberg (1911-1982), a band director for three decades: Woodruff High School, the Bradley University band, and Pekin Community High School, where he founded the Marching 100. At four, Dan "conducted" his father's band, standing in front of it with a baton
- **Start**: Piano, then a Hawaiian guitar from his grandfather; the Beatles at about 12; teenage bands The Clan and The Coachmen. At the University of Illinois he studied theatre and painting and played the Red Herring coffeehouse in Champaign, where agent Irving Azoff discovered him. His father told him to "go try it for a year"
- **Labels**: Columbia (*Home Free*, 1972), then Azoff's Full Moon label through Epic
- **Joe Walsh**: Produced *Souvenirs* (1974) and played on most of it; Fogelberg was at first wary of Walsh's hard-rock reputation. Later Walsh and his Barnstorm bandmates played on his records
- **Colorado**: Moved to the Colorado mountains in the mid-1970s, later to a ranch near Pagosa Springs with his own studio, Mountain Bird; also a house on Deer Isle, Maine
- **Death**: Diagnosed with advanced prostate cancer in 2004; died 16 December 2007 at his home on Deer Isle, aged 56. His wife Jean has since raised funds for the Prostate Cancer Foundation, including the 2017 tribute album

| Era | Releases | Sound |
|-----|----------|-------|
| 1972-1975 | *Home Free*, *Souvenirs*, *Captured Angel* | Folk and country rock: acoustic guitars, dobro and steel, fiddle, strings; "sparse, countrified folk sound" with "a distinctively more pop feel" on *Souvenirs* |
| 1977-1984 | *Nether Lands*, *Phoenix*, *The Innocent Age*, *Windows and Walls* | Orchestrated soft rock: piano ballads, orchestral arrangements (some by Dominic Frontiere, some his own), brass, flugelhorn and saxophone, stacked harmonies, synths |
| 1978, 1995 | With Tim Weisberg | Flute-led instrumental and pop-rock pieces |
| 1985 | *High Country Snows* | Bluegrass: mandolin, banjo, fiddle, Dobro, flatpicked guitar |
| 1987-2003 | *Exiles*, *The Wild Places*, *River of Souls*, *Full Circle* | Wider palette: horns and soul (*Exiles*), environmental themes, Celtic, Brazilian and African colours; more one-man recordings |

**Tempo range:** Spotify-derived readings put most of the ballads at 50-80 BPM felt (often listed double, 100-160) and the uptempo songs at about 120-145.

## "Leader of the Band"

- **Origins**: First recorded during the *Phoenix* sessions and held back; released on *The Innocent Age* (1981), and as a single in December 1981. Album version 4:48, single 4:15
- **Charts**: Hot 100 No. 9 (1982), Adult Contemporary No. 1
- **The subject**: His father, Lawrence, a high-school and college band director for three decades. The song is a son's thanks to the father who taught him music; its best-known line calls the singer "the living legacy to the leader of the band"
- **Arrangement**: Dan Fogelberg's voice and acoustic guitars, with a brass quintet arranged by Glen Spreen ("french-horn heavy", per PopMatters). The record ends with an excerpt of Sousa's march "The Washington Post", arranged by Lawrence Fogelberg and played by the UCLA Band. No drums or strings are credited
- **His father's response**: "I wasn't supposed to hear it. I've been breaking up ever since" (Peoria Journal Star, March 1982). He died that August. Fogelberg: "I was so gratified that I was able to give him that song before he passed on"
- **Legacy**: PopMatters called it possibly "the definitive 'father' song". Covered as tributes to their own fathers by Lucie Arnaz, Regine Velasquez and Zac Brown; the Mercey Brothers' version reached No. 9 on the Canadian country chart
- **Register**: Warm, grateful and openhearted rather than mournful; sung plainly over acoustic guitars, building to the brass

## Musical Analysis

### Voice and Harmonies

A high, clear tenor, "quavering" when young (Rolling Stone, 1974), with harmonies he stacked himself, often "four or five parts at once". Cashbox (1975): "a natural in the area of laidback". Later in life his voice roughened.

### Guitar

Acoustic guitar is the centre of most songs (he favoured a Martin D-41 and a "D modal" tuning, per a fan-hosted interview). His principle: "it's not what you play, it's what you don't play." He credited Eric Clapton and B.B. King for his electric playing.

### One-Man Recording

He played many instruments himself: guitars, bass, keyboards and synths (Minimoog, Prophet-5), lap steel, electric sitar, hammered dulcimer, bowed psaltery. Early ("Hickory Grove", 1970) and late (*Full Circle*, *Love in Time*) he played everything. The exception: "I can play a lot of instruments, but drums is not one of them."

### Orchestration

Strings, brass and woodwinds are central to the late-1970s and 1980s records: Dominic Frontiere's arrangements on *Nether Lands*, his own on *Phoenix*, Glen Spreen's brass on "Leader of the Band". He named Copland, Grieg, Tchaikovsky, Chopin and Debussy as influences, and built "Same Old Lang Syne" from a Tchaikovsky melody.

### Themes

- **His father and family**: "Leader of the Band"
- **Home and leaving it**: Peoria and the Illinois river ("I knew the river was a conscious metaphor for my escape from Peoria")
- **Nature and the mountains**: Colorado winters, ecology (*Nether Lands*, *The Wild Places*)
- **Love and nostalgia**: "Longer", "Same Old Lang Syne", *The Innocent Age*
- "I know metaphysical songs aren't going to sell on the radio"

### Genre Classification

- **Singer-songwriter / soft rock**: The primary label
- **Folk rock / country rock**: The early albums
- **Orchestral pop**: *Nether Lands*, *The Innocent Age*
- **Bluegrass**: *High Country Snows*

## Reception and Influence

| Album | Selected verdicts |
|-------|-------------------|
| *Souvenirs* | Christgau C−; Rolling Stone on his "high, quavering tenor" |
| *Nether Lands* | Popdose (2012): returning to it, "amazed" |
| *Twin Sons of Different Mothers* | Dave Marsh: "smooth post-rock cocktail-lounge Muzak"; Blender named it his worst |
| *The Innocent Age* | AllMusic: "beautifully orchestrated, imaginatively conceived"; Rolling Stone Album Guide: "banal and overblown" |
| *The Wild Places* | Chicago Tribune: for listeners "wearing crystals while riding in an elevator" |

Garth Brooks: "That was an artist who changed my life." The 2017 tribute album gathered Brooks, the Eagles, Michael McDonald, Amy Grant and Vince Gill, Jimmy Buffett, Boz Scaggs, Train and Zac Brown. "Same Old Lang Syne" has been covered by Jonathan Coulton and the Backstreet Boys.
